# Ordinary squared-slope mass and its spectrum

**PROPOSED.** The finite constructions concern the positive integer shortcut map `T(2m)=m`, `T(2m+1)=3m+2`. The statements about failure of universal convergence are implications. They neither identify a bad start nor rule one out.

## A single rational array on the actual graph

Join every positive integer `v` to `T(v)`. Give each edge `v -> T(v)` the squared slope `w(v)=1/4` at an even input and `w(v)=9/4` at an odd input. A mass `a` on such an edge obeys `a(v)=w(v)a(T(v))`. Set `a(0)=0`. Set all masses in the component of 1 to zero. This component consists exactly of the starts visiting 1.

In any other component containing a cycle, delete the outgoing edge of its least cycle vertex `r`. This vertex is odd, since an even vertex has a smaller successor. Calibrate there by `a(r)=4^(-ceil(log_2 r))` and propagate the edge rule. In an acyclic component use its least positive vertex `r` as the calibration site, with the same mass and all edges retained. An acyclic connected component of the undirected functional graph is a tree. Cutting the stated edge also gives a tree in a cyclic component (an edge on a loop or two-cycle is counted with its original orientation). Thus propagation is consistent. Every nonzero coefficient belongs to the multiplicative group generated by 4 and 9. The construction gives the global, exact relation `4a(2n)=a(n)`, including in the components in which one odd cycle edge was removed.

For `H>=1` use vertices `1,...,H` and retain just the directed edges whose two endpoints are present. Put zero on a component containing 1. For each of the other components use its least cycle phase and delete its outgoing edge if the truncated graph has a directed cycle. Otherwise anchor at the least vertex of that truncated component. Apply the same edge rule. For fixed `n` the rational output `a_H(n)` is eventually **exactly** `a(n)`. On a cyclic component the finite paths to its cycle and the cycle itself eventually fit, and the anchor then stays fixed. On an acyclic component the finite path joining `n` to its true minimum eventually fits. No smaller vertex can enter after that. From then on the same finite path computes precisely the same rational. This stabilization makes no assertion about the size of a sufficient cutoff.

For a finite simple directed path `v_0,...,v_k` put `D=prod_{i odd input}(1+1/(3v_i))`. With `A=prod slope(v_i)`, the exact endpoint law is `v_k=v_0 A D`. The ordinary reciprocal packing bound for finite simple paths supplies one constant `D_*` with `1<=D<=D_*`, independent of the start and path. Indeed T-A3-651 on the main checkout, `research/astra-three-routes/pass3/FINITE_RECORDS.md`, equations (1)–(2), gives `sum 1/v <= 200` on the odd sites of every finite simple shortcut path. Thus `D_* = exp(200/3)` suffices by `log(1+x)<=x`. The product of tail-free edge weights along the segment is precisely `A²`. Consequently `b(n):=n²a(n)` is in `{0} ∪ [1/(4D_*²),D_*²]`: for an acyclic component, direct paths from the calibration site and `n` to their first common point and divide the two product identities. In a rooted cycle component use the single path from `n` to `r`, stopping at its first arrival. The zero and unit endpoint conventions include the empty path. The same bounds hold for each finite component by following the paths to a common sink or to the deleted-edge root. In particular `A(z)=sum_{n>=1} a(n)z^n` is holomorphic in the disk and continuous on its closure. If nonzero, it has convergence radius exactly 1 by the even ray through any nonzero term.

The ordinary fixed-basin block theorem strengthens the general spectral arguments below to a full natural boundary at every positive real exponent. See [the complete strengthening](BASIN_BLOCK_BOUNDARY.md).

## Perfect singular spectrum

If `a` is nonzero, the set of points of the unit circle through which `A` admits no holomorphic continuation contains a nonempty perfect set. This consequence uses ordinary support and the uniform mass gap. A conjectural upper bound on this singularity set would need independent justification.

To prove it let `B=(z d/dz)^2 A`, with bounded coefficients `b(n)`. A regular arc of `A` is regular for `B`. Let `t` be any two-sided right limit of `b(n)` along dates `N_j -> infinity` (put `b(n)=0` at negative indices before shifting). Its bounded coefficients define a distribution on the circle. On a regular arc the boundary distribution of `B` is an analytic function. Multiplying that boundary distribution by `exp(-i N_j theta)` shifts its coefficients; the result tends distributionally to the Fourier distribution of `t` by dominated summation against the rapidly decaying coefficients of smooth tests. On the arc it tends weakly to zero by integration by parts, so the Fourier support of `t` is contained in the closed singularity set of `A` (up to the harmless choice of Fourier sign). This inclusion also holds for the Beurling spectrum on the discrete group of integers: a smooth cutoff identically one near a frequency off the distributional support annihilates `t` by convolution, and its Fourier coefficients are in `ell^1`.

If this closed set were countable, Loomis's theorem on bounded uniformly continuous scalar functions on locally compact abelian groups would make `t` Bohr almost periodic. The support indicator of `t` is then almost periodic and binary: uniform almost periods with error less than the gap are exact periods. In particular it is periodic. Every two-sided right limit of the binary support indicator of `b` lifts to a right limit of `b` by compact diagonal extraction, and the gap preserves its support in the limit. Loomis’s theorem is at Ann. Math. 72 (1960), 362–368, opening theorem. Chen, Jian and Zhong, *Loomis Type Theorem for Bounded Sequences*, Theorem 7, gives the scalar discrete version explicitly.

For completeness an aperiodic tail of a binary sequence supplies two right limits agreeing at all negative indices and differing at index zero. At every fixed memory length `k`, beyond every source cutoff there must be two equal length-`k` histories with distinct next symbols. Otherwise the tail follows a deterministic rule on finitely many length-`k` states and becomes periodic. Take `k` tending to infinity and pass to simultaneous two-sided limits. Two periodic two-sided sequences equal on a negative half-line agree everywhere, a contradiction. Thus a countable singular set would force the support to be eventually periodic. A nonempty proper bad support contains infinitely many points by doubling. If ultimately periodic it would contain an entire infinite positive arithmetic progression. Monks proved that every infinite progression meets the component of 1, which is disjoint from this support. A countable singular set is therefore impossible. The compact perfect-set theorem finishes the argument.

The weighted physical series need not have a natural boundary by this argument: the uncountable closed set is not proved to be the entire circle. The characteristic series of the basin of 1 has its own established Pólya–Carlson dichotomy in Bell–Lagarias. The physical masses here range over infinitely many ordinary rational values and in general have unbounded denominators even on a single dyadic ray.

## A natural boundary for almost every exponent

For a real exponent `s>0` retain the physical edge law by using

\[
 M_s(z)=\sum_{a(n)>0} a(n)^{s/2}z^n,\qquad B_s(z)=\sum_{b(n)>0} b(n)^{s/2}z^n .
\]

If a bad ordinary component exists, the entire unit circle is a natural boundary of `M_s` for every positive real `s` outside a countable set. More precisely, for each fixed open arc the exponents permitting analytic continuation throughout that arc are contained in the zero set of one nonzero entire function of `s`. The same theorem applies when masses of any nonempty collection of bad components are retained and the rest set to zero, since the support is still a nonempty union of components disjoint from 1. Each `M_s` has radius 1 by the dyadic rays and the uniform estimate. The argument in this section alone does not resolve the exponent `s=2`. On the actual graph the long-block argument cited above removes the entire exceptional set.

Fix an arc `I`. The two binary right limits constructed above agree on the negative half-line and differ at index zero. At least one has nonzero Fourier distribution on `I`. Otherwise their difference is the nonzero Fourier distribution `W` of a bounded two-sided sequence `(w_j)` with `w_j=0` for `j<0`, supported on the complement of `I`. Its Cauchy transform on the disk is `sum_{j>=0}w_j z^j` (choose the Fourier sign accordingly), while on the exterior it is zero by expansion in `z^(-1)` and the missing negative coefficients. Since `W` is supported away from any compact subarc of `I`, the kernel analytically connects these two branches there. Identity of holomorphic functions gives `w_0=0`, a contradiction.

Lift such a binary right limit to the right limit `(t_j)` of `b` along the same subsequence. Choose a smooth test supported in `I` pairing nontrivially with that binary limit, and denote its rapidly decaying Fourier weights by `c_j`. Set

\[
    F_I(s)=\sum_{j:t_j>0} c_j\exp((s/2)\log t_j).
\]

Since `t_j` is bounded above and away from zero on its support, this series converges normally on compact subsets of the parameter plane. The entire function satisfies `F_I(0) != 0`. For real `s>0` the coefficients `1_{t_j>0} t_j^(s/2)` are right limits of the coefficients of `B_s` by continuity across the separated range. The preceding distribution argument proves `F_I(s)=0` if `B_s` continues analytically through `I`.

Analytic continuation of `M_s` through `I` entails continuation of `B_s` there. For an integer `q>s` apply the Euler derivative `D=z(d/dz)`, then use

\[
  B_s(z)=\Gamma(q-s)^{-1}\int_0^1
    (\log(1/t))^{q-s-1} (D^qM_s)(tz)\,dt/t .
\]

Integration on each monomial gives the identity inside the disk. At a point of the arc choose a small complex neighborhood and `t_0<1` so that `tz` is inside the unit disk for `0<=t<=t_0`, and inside the continued neighborhood for `t_0<=t<=1`; the neighborhoods may overlap. At zero the derivative is `O(t)` and the real weight is integrable at 1. The integral therefore gives a holomorphic continuation to a neighborhood of that point. This proves the isolated-zero constraint. Taking a countable basis of arcs proves the assertion about the entire circle.

This argument selects its test from genuine right limits of one physical array. It does not assert a time uniform rate for the finite graph approximations. The exceptional set depends on the array and is not effectively identified.
