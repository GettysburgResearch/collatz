# Multiplicative rank on actual integer cycles and escaping orbits

**PROPOSED written theorem.** A nontrivial positive integer cycle has multiplicative affine rank **at least 32** among its odd states. That is, for any odd state $x_0$ on it, the ratios $x/x_0$ generate a multiplicative group of rank at least 32. In particular at least 32 distinct primes divide the odd states. The generators and their prime divisors can be arbitrarily large. The result uses a numerical period lower bound from Hercher and Barina. Barina's verification together with the smaller, independently enclosed Farey bound below gives rank at least 31.

More generally, fix a coset $t\Gamma$ in the positive rationals, with $\Gamma$ of multiplicative rank $r$. Across **all** positive ordinary odd transitions
\[
                  2^a y=3x+1,\qquad a=v_2(3x+1),
\]
the number of distinct source values $x$ with $3\nmid x$ and both $x,y\in t\Gamma$ is at most
\[
                         B(r)=2^{r+1}(r+1).                       \tag{1}
\]
There is no restriction on $a$. [The sparse-subset extension](WINDOWS.md) treats endpoints separated by arbitrarily many divisions by two and a bounded number of intervening odd steps. [The joint-target calculation](HEADS.md) applies the rank method to PR197's exact affine lines.

## Turning a physical odd step into a Pell equation

We can take $t$ to be any actual marked endpoint in the coset. Prime valuation vectors identify positive rationals with the direct sum of copies of $\mathbb Z$ indexed by primes. A rank-$r$ subgroup is finitely generated: choose $r$ linearly independent elements spanning its rational vector space. All group elements have support on their finite union of prime divisors, and a subgroup of a finitely generated free abelian group is free abelian and finitely generated. Its image in positive rational squareclasses has size at most $2^r$.

Set $D$ equal to the squarefree part of $3x(3x+1)$, and put $X=6x+1$. There is a positive integer $V$ with
\[
             X^2-DV^2=1,\qquad DV^2=12\,2^a xy.                 \tag{2}
\]
Indeed $X^2-1=4(3x)(3x+1)$. The consecutive pair $(3x,3x+1)$ is the classical Størmer–Lehmer input. Here $3$ occurs exactly once in its product, so $3\mid D$ and $D>1$. Its rational squareclass is $[3\cdot 2^a xy]$. Since $x,y\in t\Gamma$, the common factor $t^2$ disappears. There are at most $2^{r+1}$ possible $D$ as $a,x,y$ vary. The usual finiteness theorem for fixed smoothness primes would retain all primes in the generators. We require a count independent of their number. [D. H. Lehmer, *On a problem of Størmer*, §§2–3](https://doi.org/10.1215/ijm/1256067456)

Fix one such $D$. Write $u+v\sqrt D$ for the least solution greater than one with positive integer Pell coordinates and norm one. Every positive solution in integer coordinates is $(u+v\sqrt D)^j=X_j+V_j\sqrt D$, $j\ge1$. For completeness, division by the fundamental unit preserves integral coordinates and norm; a positive real unit strictly between one and the fundamental one has positive coordinates and contradicts minimality. This proves the parameterization even when the full ring of integers admits half-integer units. Put $P=2u$ and $U_j=V_j/v$. Then $U_0=0,U_1=1$, $U_{j+2}=P U_{j+1}-U_j$. This real Lucas pair has product one, positive nonequal roots and integer trace $P\ge4$. For every $j>2$ it has an **odd** prime dividing $U_j$ and none of $U_1,\ldots,U_{j-1}$. Carmichael's theorem as proved by Yabuta gives a new prime except possibly at $j=1,2,6$; the exceptional Fibonacci parameters at 12 have product $-1$. Two already divides $U_2=P$. At $j=6$ take any prime factor of $P^2-3>1$. It is odd. It differs from three because $u^2\equiv1\pmod3$. The first five positive terms are $1,P,P^2-1,P(P^2-2),P^4-3P^2+1$. None is divisible by that prime, whereas $U_6=P(P^2-1)(P^2-3)$ is. We use only first appearance in the sequence. [Minoru Yabuta, *A simple proof of Carmichael's theorem on primitive divisors*, Theorem 1](https://www.fq.math.ca/Scanned/39-5/yabuta.pdf)

Let $j_1<\cdots<j_m$ be any finite set of indices arising from our marked edges at this $D$. Both 1 and 2 cannot occur: if $X_1=6x+1$ occurs, $X_1$ is odd, hence $X_2=2X_1^2-1\equiv1\pmod4$, while every accepted $X$ is $3\pmod4$. Thus $j_i>2$ for $i\ge2$. For each of those indices take a fresh odd prime. After deleting the 2-coordinate of the prime valuation vectors, the $m-1$ ratios $V_{j_i}/V_{j_1}=U_{j_i}/U_{j_1}$ are multiplicatively independent. At the fresh prime for the largest-index ratio in any proposed relation, all earlier ratios have zero valuation and this ratio has positive valuation. The common multiplier $v$ has already cancelled.

But after deleting the 2-coordinate, twice each ratio's valuation vector is, by (2), the sum of the vectors of $x_i/x_1$ and $y_i/y_1$ projected in the same way. This puts all $m-1$ vectors in a rational vector space of dimension at most $r$. Hence $m\le r+1$. The Pell index at fixed $D$ determines $X$, hence the actual integer $x$ and its unique successor. Summing over $D$ proves (1), also for an arbitrary infinite collection by applying it first to every finite subcollection.

## Complete returns and companions

Every odd state following an odd step is a unit at three, because $2^a y=3x+1$. Hence all odd states on a positive cycle meet the $3\nmid x$ restriction. A primitive cycle with $p$ odd states has $p$ different source transitions. If $m$ of its odd states miss a chosen rank-$r$ coset, at most $2m$ edges have an endpoint missing. Thus
\[
                         p-2m\le B(r).                            \tag{3}
\]
Barina reports verification of convergence through $2^{71}$, which contains $3\cdot2^{69}$. Hercher, Corollary 29 and Remark 30, gives $p>P_0=137\,500\,000\,000$ from the latter verification threshold. His $K$ counts odd members, while his $m$ counts local minima. The author's corrigendum repairs a step of Theorem 21; Corollary 29 separately uses a search based on Lemma 26. These are published computational inputs, with no repetition of either external search here. Now $B(31)=137\,438\,953\,472<P_0$. Equation (3) excludes every complete rank-31 return and bounds the missing proportion below by $(1-B(r)/P_0)/2$ whenever $B(r)<P_0$. At rank 30 the lower bound exceeds one quarter. At rank 31 it exceeds $1/5000$. [Barina, §6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11227-025-07337-0) · [Hercher, Corollary 29 and Remark 30](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/journals/JIS/VOL26/Hercher/hercher5.pdf) · [June 2026 corrigendum](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/journals/JIS/VOL26/Hercher/corrigendum.pdf)

There is also a weaker floor using Barina alone. If $A$ is the sum of the $p$ stripping exponents then the cycle product puts $A/p$ between $\log_2 3$ and $\log_2(3+2^{-71})$, inclusive at the upper end. These lie strictly between $103768467013/65470613321$ and $10439860591/6586818670$. The endpoints have determinant one, so $p\ge72\,057\,431\,991>B(30)$. [check.py](check.py) checks these strict inclusions using rational geometric tail bounds for $\log$, without floating point.

For any one positive source, suppose arbitrarily long windows of successive odd states each have more than $1-1/5000$ of their visits in a rank-at-most-31 coset; the coset may change with the window. Then the source **reaches 1**. On an escape all these states are distinct; in an $N$-state segment with $m$ misses, at least $N-1-2m$ consecutive pairs lie in the coset. At most one additional pair can start at a multiple of three, so (1) forbids such arbitrarily long windows. On a nontrivial periodic tail, the fixed source has finite preperiod and fixed period. Uniformly over all choices of coset and starting mark, the fraction of misses on long windows is bounded below asymptotically by $(1-B(31)/P_0)/2>1/5000$ using complete laps in (3). The remaining possible tail is the fixed odd state 1 and it satisfies the displayed condition. At rank at most 30 the same argument works with inclusion fraction $3/4$.

This supplies the successful dyadic companion on this class without a preassigned candidate: iterate the physical source to 1, then propagate $\mathcal D(1)=9$ backward using $\mathcal D(x)=e+2^{1+e}\mathcal D(Tx)/3$ at each shortcut bit $e$. The output belongs to $\mathbb Z[1/3]$. An arbitrary fixed input is not known to satisfy the coset concentration premise. Fibers from near-linear source families with the same target yield arbitrarily many different initial integers and typically a growing carry. The fixed endpoint bounds its own rank only. It gives no rank bound on intermediate odd states of one fixed path. [PR193, companion splitting and supplied-candidate comparison, frozen source](https://github.com/GettysburgResearch/collatz/blob/cc800b9041efdeee43bb89df21088a81e1f44a74/research/astra-global-resolution-20261124/arithmetic/README.md)

## The stronger observable and the two fixed-endpoint arms

The proof of (1) actually requires only that the **products** $xy$ lie in one coset $t\Gamma$. Their squareclasses range over $[t]$ times the image of $\Gamma$, and the ratios of products in (2) already belong to $\Gamma$. Thus every nontrivial primitive cycle has affine rank at least 32 even for its list of adjacent products $x_i x_{i+1}$. The complete-return lower bound also excludes arbitrarily long windows with more than $B(31)/P_0$ of their **transitions** in a rank-31 product coset by a uniform strict margin (on a transient tail, discard the finite preperiod and partial laps as above). For state occupancy the factor two in (3) is still necessary in that argument.

There is a separate, unconditional statement for the ratio-five arms. Write $y=\operatorname{Syr}(x), z=\operatorname{Syr}(5x)$ at an ordinary positive odd $x$, and let $a,b$ respectively be the two stripping exponents. Then
\[
      X=(15x+3)/2,\quad X-1=2^{b-1}z,\quad X+1=5\,2^{a-1}y,
      \qquad X^2-1=5\,2^{a+b-2}yz.                              \tag{4}
\]
For any $t\Gamma$ of rank $r$, at most $B(r)$ **distinct ordinary sources** $x$ can have $yz\in t\Gamma$. Indeed $X^2-1$ lies strictly between successive squares for $X>1$, so its squarefree part $D$ exceeds one. Its squareclass is now a fixed translate of the product of $[2]$ and the allowed product squareclasses, again at most $2^{r+1}$ choices. At fixed $D$, indices 1 and 2 cannot both occur because $3\mid X$ but $3\mid X_1$ would imply $X_2\equiv-1\pmod3$. The earlier first-appearance argument at index 6 still works when $3\mid P$: the odd number $(P^2-3)/3>1$ is a unit at three since $P\ge6$ then, and any of its primes divides none of $U_1,\dots,U_5$. Thus every index after the first selected has a fresh odd prime. After deleting the 2-coordinate, twice each valuation difference equals that of the corresponding product quotient $yz/(y_0z_0)$. This proves the same count, with no control assumed on either successor's individual factors.

For a collection of $M$ such source pairs, the rank of the group generated by the first-successor product ratios is at least $\log_2 M-O(\log\log(2M))$ as $M\to\infty$, by inverting $M\le B(r)$. Apply this to the [fixed ordinary endpoint fibers](../pressure/README.md), conditional on their specified analytic inputs. At those very sources both final endpoints can be fixed while the products of their **first actual odd successors** must have growing rank. The growth holds for each individual fiber before any averaging. Hence simply propagating the rank-zero final product backward cannot control the corresponding earlier products. The rank theorem applies to any distinct positive odd sources, including ones that all reach trivial orbits, and does not establish a failed merge or a universally successful one.
