# Sparse return spectra along an ordinary Collatz trajectory

**PROPOSED.** The general arithmetic lemma below imports the Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt theorem on nondegenerate equations in a multiplicative group. A sharper cardinality bound uses Evertse's 1995 theorem for S-units. The cases needed for the explicit two-bit and three-bit trajectory bounds have elementary proofs. This note does not prove Collatz, rule out all positive cycles, or claim an external priority result for the S-unit argument.

## 1. What changes relative to the source packets

PR #141, frozen at `5025ef464d4b6d4f4a412a3e33c3477de68dfa8b`, proves a fixed-source separated-carry identity and a finite core for each permanently bounded popcount, in `research/ay-persistent-20261031/PROOF.md`, HC-1–HC-3. Its sparse-source descent result is a different statement: the initial source can descend to a denser integer.

PR #145, frozen at `dd245a712f985909f541590e3f6c179b7ba011e4`, proves the uniform binary-run occupancy bound

\[
 O_R\bigl(N^{1-1/(2R)}(1+\log N)^{2R}\bigr)
\]

in `research/binary-run-occupancy/README.md`. That bound also applies to a popcount cap by treating each set bit as a run. Its proof samples homogeneous multiplication modulo a power of two.

This note instead proves that the possible *time differences* between two sparse homogeneous values form a finite set, uniformly over the ordinary starting value. The same original-source carry identity then gives:

* a finite allowable spectrum of early sparse returns, followed by a forbidden interval until the carry cut closes;
* the sharper popcount estimate `O_S(N^(1-1/S))`, with no logarithmic factor, in any segment of `N` distinct ordinary odd states;
* elementary explicit versions for popcounts two and three;
* a concrete counterexample to extending the spectrum past the lifetime of its original cut.

All clocks in this note count **odd events**, not shortcut steps or literal rewrite steps. Write

\[
 U(n)=\frac{3n+1}{2^{v_2(3n+1)}}\quad(n\text{ positive odd}),
 \qquad s(n)=\operatorname{popcount}_2(n).
\]

We extend `U(1)=1` only in the shadow arithmetic. An actual window with pairwise distinct states contains at most one occurrence of 1. A separated actual source stays above 1 throughout the indicated window.

## 2. The homogeneous spectrum is finite

For an integer `S>=1`, define

\[
 E_S=\{h\ge0:\ \exists u\ge1\text{ odd},\ s(u)\le S,
                              \ s(3^h u)\le S\}.
\]

**SR-1.** `E_S` is finite. In particular there are constants `K_S=|E_S|` and `H_S=max E_S`, depending only on `S`, such that every homogeneous orbit `3^j u` has at most `K_S` visits to popcount at most `S`; all its such visits lie within an interval of length `H_S`. The initial `u` need not be sparse for this last assertion.

### Proof of finiteness, including the uniformity in u

For `h>0` in `E_S`, write the two binary expansions as

\[
 \sum_{i=1}^{p}2^{a_i}=3^h\sum_{j=1}^{q}2^{b_j},
 \qquad 1\le p,q\le S.
\]

Choose a minimal nonempty vanishing subsum after moving the right side to the left. It contains terms from both sides: all terms on either one side have the same sign. Rename its numbers of terms `p,q`. If `p=q=1`, unique factorization forces `h=0`, contrary to our choice. Thus `m=p+q-1>=2`.

Normalize by one left term `2^a`. The resulting equation is

\[
 \sum_{j=1}^{q}3^h2^{b_j-a}
  -\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}2^{a_i-a}=1.                    \tag{1}
\]

It is nondegenerate: a zero subsum on the left would be a proper zero subsum of the chosen minimal relation. For fixed `p,q`, its coefficient vector consists of `q` entries `+1`, followed by `p-1` entries `-1`. Its variable vector belongs to the fixed group generated by the `m` coordinate powers of two and the vector that is 3 in the first `q` coordinates and 1 elsewhere. This group has rank `m+1`; **no coefficient depends on u or h**.

Theorem 1.1 of Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt bounds the number of nondegenerate solutions in a group of rank `r` by

\[
 A(m,r)=\exp\bigl((6m)^{3m}(r+1)\bigr).
\]

Every vector in (1) determines `h` by the 3-adic valuation of its first coordinate. Taking the finite union over `1<=p,q<=S` proves finiteness. One explicit, deliberately very loose cardinality bound is

\[
 |E_S|\le 1+
 \sum_{\substack{1\le p,q\le S\\p+q\ge3}}
 \exp\bigl((6(p+q-1))^{3(p+q-1)}(p+q+1)\bigr).       \tag{2}
\]

The initial 1 allows `h=0`. This is a bound for the **number** of possible exponents, not their largest value. The quoted theorem does not make (2) a numerical upper bound for `H_S`.

For the final assertion, if the orbit has a sparse visit, let `j_0` be its first. Then `v=3^{j_0}u` is odd and sparse. Every later sparse visit at `j` gives `j-j_0 in E_S`. This proves both assertions. QED.

**External input.** J.-H. Evertse, H. P. Schlickewei and W. M. Schmidt, *Linear equations in variables which lie in a multiplicative group*, Annals of Mathematics 155 (2002), 807–836, Theorem 1.1, printed p. 808. The statement and definition of nondegeneracy were checked in the [author preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0409604), pp. 1–2; [publisher record](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2002/155-3/p04), DOI [10.2307/3062133](https://doi.org/10.2307/3062133). The external theorem is imported, not re-proved or formally verified here. The application above is a standard minimal-subsum use of it; no novelty is claimed for this general number-theoretic mechanism.

### A much smaller bound from the fixed set of prime places

**SR-1Q.** The same proof gives, for `S>=2`,

\[
 \boxed{|E_S|\le\mathcal K_S
 :=1+S^2\bigl(2^{35}(2S-1)^2\bigr)^{3(2S-1)^3}.}       \tag{2a}
\]

In particular `log(mathcal K_S)=O(S^3 log S)`, substantially smaller than the bound obtained by using general finite-rank groups. This is an explicit cardinality bound, still not an upper bound for the largest exponent `H_S`.

Every normalized variable in (1) is a rational unit outside the fixed set of places `Sigma={infinity,2,3}`. Evertse's Theorem 3 states that an equation in `m` such variables, with fixed nonzero coefficients and no vanishing nonempty subsum, has at most

\[
 (2^{35}m^2)^{m^3|\Sigma|}=(2^{35}m^2)^{3m^3}
\]

solutions. Its nondegeneracy condition was already checked in the proof of SR-1. The same first-coordinate valuation recovers `h`. There are at most `S^2` choices of `p,q`, and each has `m=p+q-1<=2S-1`; enlarging each summand to the largest displayed bound proves (2a). The `p=q=1` case only contributes the separately included exponent zero.

The sharper external input is J.-H. Evertse, *The number of solutions of decomposable form equations*, Theorem 3, [author preprint](https://pub.math.leidenuniv.nl/~evertsejh/95-decforms.pdf), printed p. 7. The definition of the set of places, the nondegeneracy condition, and the numerical theorem were independently inspected. This quantitative sharpening was identified during the root agent's literature check, then reconstructed separately here. It preserves the minimal-subsum argument and its source credit; it supplies no new height bound for the solutions of the external equation.

## 3. Two complete elementary spectra

**SR-2.** `E_1={0}` and `E_2={0,1,2}`. For positive `h`, all pairs of odd integers with both popcounts at most two and ratio `3^h` are

\[
 (u,3^hu,h)=(1,3,1),\ (1,9,2),\ (3,9,1).
\]

### Proof

An odd one-bit integer is 1, giving `E_1`. If `u=1` and `3^h=2^e+1`, the cases `e=1,2` give only `(h,e)=(1,1)`. If `e>=3`, reduction modulo 8 makes `h` even. The two positive even factors

\[
 (3^{h/2}-1)(3^{h/2}+1)=2^e
\]

are powers of two differing by two, so they are 2 and 4. Hence `(h,e)=(2,3)`.

Otherwise `u=2^d+1` and `3^hu=2^e+1`, with `e>d>=1`. The elementary identity for `gcd(2^d+1,2^e+1)` implies that `d` divides `e` and `k=e/d` is odd. For completeness, if `g=gcd(d,e)`, that gcd equals `2^g+1` when both `d/g` and `e/g` are odd, and equals 1 otherwise. The identity follows by the Euclidean algorithm on the exponents, with the sign changing upon every subtraction of an odd multiple. Since `2^d+1` divides `2^e+1`, the asserted divisibility and odd quotient follow.

Put `x=2^d`. Then `k>=3` and

\[
 3^h=\frac{x^k+1}{x+1}.
\]

If `d` is even, `x=1 mod 3` and this odd alternating geometric sum is `1 mod 3`, impossible. If `d` is odd, the elementary lifting identity

\[
 v_3(x^k+1)-v_3(x+1)=v_3(k)\quad(k\text{ odd},\ 3\mid x+1)
\]

gives `3^h=3^{v_3(k)}<=k`. This lifting identity follows by the binomial expansion for a factor of 3 in `k`, followed by the geometric sum for a factor coprime to 3. But `(x^k+1)/(x+1)>= (2^k+1)/3>k` for odd `k>=5`. At `k=3`, the quotient is `x^2-x+1`, which is at most 3 only for `x=2`. This gives `(u,3^hu,h)=(3,9,1)`. All displayed pairs occur. QED.

## 4. A sparse return must respect the original carry cut

Let a sparse positive odd `n` have at least two set bits. Select any gap `d` between consecutive set-bit positions and split

\[
 n=2^D u+v,
 \qquad u,v\text{ positive odd},\quad
 0<v<2^{D-d+1}.
\]

Here `D` is the position of the lower set bit of the upper block. Put

\[
 J=\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor.
\]

**SR-3.** If `s(n)<=S`, then every index `0<=j<=J` at which the *actual* state `U^j(n)` has popcount at most `S` belongs to `E_(S-1)`. Consequently no such return occurs at any `H_(S-1)<j<=J`.

### Proof of the fixed-source identity and implication

Follow the shadow orbit `v_j=U^j(v)`, retaining `U(1)=1`, and let `A_j` be its total valuation through `j` odd events. Set `P_j=2^{A_j}v_j`. Then

\[
 P_0=v,\qquad P_{j+1}=3P_j+2^{A_j},\qquad
 0<P_j\le4^jv.
\]

For `j<=J`, the gap bound gives `4^jv<2^D`; thus `A_j<D`. An induction using the strictly smaller valuation of the lower numerator gives

\[
 U^j(n)=2^{D-A_j}3^ju+v_j,\qquad
 0<v_j<2^{D-A_j}.                              \tag{3}
\]

The upper and lower binary blocks are disjoint, so

\[
 s(U^j(n))=s(3^ju)+s(v_j).
\]

The lower block contributes at least one bit. Also `s(u)<=S-1` at time zero. A later full state with at most `S` bits must therefore have `s(3^ju)<=S-1`, proving `j in E_(S-1)`. The whole calculation follows the original `n`: no replacement source or favorable completion is chosen. QED.

In particular, a source with at most two bits has **no** two-bit return at `1<=j<=J`. A source with at most three bits has **no** three-bit return at `3<=j<=J`. Taking the largest gap makes `d>=ceil(floor(log_2 n)/(S-1))`, so the forbidden interval extends a distance proportional to `log n`, beyond the fixed initial spectrum.

The strict separation guard matters. For `n=5=2^2+1`, the cut has `J=0`; at the next odd event `U(5)=1`, and the disjoint formula has already failed. In a less terminal example,

\[
 257\longmapsto193\longmapsto145\longmapsto109\longmapsto41.
\]

The original gap is 8, hence `J=3`. The three-bit visits at clocks 0, 1 and 2 respect `E_2`. The three-bit return at clock 4 is outside the original cut's lifetime. Thus the proposed strengthening “all future sparse returns use the initial finite spectrum” is **REFUTED**, already by this ordinary convergent trajectory.

## 5. Uniform occupancy on distinct ordinary segments

**SR-4.** Let `S>=2`, `L>=1`, and let `x_0,...,x_(N-1)` be `N` pairwise distinct positive odd states on one forward orbit. Then

\[
 \#\{j<N:s(x_j)\le S\}
 \le \sum_{r=1}^S(2L)^{r-1}
       + |E_{S-1}|\left\lceil\frac NL\right\rceil.       \tag{4}
\]

In particular the count is `O_S(N^(1-1/S))`, uniformly in the source and the position of the segment. No convergence hypothesis is used.

### Proof

An odd integer with exactly `r` bits has a unique list of `r-1` positive adjacent gaps. If all gaps are at most `2L`, there are exactly `(2L)^(r-1)` choices. Pairwise distinctness bounds the number of such visits by the sum in (4).

Scan the time interval from left to right. On encountering a sparse state with a gap greater than `2L`, reserve its next `L` positions, truncated at the end, and resume after them. Such a gap has `J>=L`, so SR-3 bounds the number of sparse visits within this window by `|E_(S-1)|`. The reserved starts are at least `L` apart; there are at most `ceil(N/L)` of them. Every sparse visit not counted in a reserved window has only short gaps, and was counted in the first term. This proves (4).

Take `L=max(1,floor(N^(1/S)))`. The first term is `O_S(L^(S-1))`, and the second is `O_S(N/L+1)`, proving the stated order. The constant may be explicitly bounded through (2a), though the result remains numerically large. QED.

The fully elementary instances are

\[
 \#\{j<N:s(x_j)\le2\}\le 1+2L+\lceil N/L\rceil,
\]

\[
 \#\{j<N:s(x_j)\le3\}\le 1+2L+4L^2+3\lceil N/L\rceil.
\]

Every nonperiodic ordinary positive orbit has distinct odd states. Thus its low-popcount event times have upper Banach density zero, with the sharper displayed rates. The qualitative zero-density consequence was already supplied by PR #145; the difference here is the exponent, absence of the logarithmic factor, and the explicit sparse-return spectrum.

## 6. What a full proof would still need

This proof gives **upper** bounds on sparse visits. It supplies no positive lower frequency of such visits on an escaping orbit, no global cap on popcount, and no bound on the new cut after the previous one closes. The concrete `257 -> ... -> 41` control shows why discarding carry collisions is invalid. A new cut can have a different upper block and an uncharged clock; repeated re-selection has no well-founded budget here.

Consequently these inequalities are compatible with an orbit that becomes progressively dense in binary. They also do not exclude arbitrary nontrivial cycles: the distinctness hypothesis applies to one primitive tour, not to infinitely many repeated tours. Closing the proposed carry route would require a genuine global budget on cut closure and replacement, or an independent theorem forcing more sparse visits than (4) permits. Neither has been proved in this pass.

## 7. Replay and evidence boundary

`check_sparse_returns.py` uses Python integers and the literal odd map. It tests the complete elementary two-bit pair classification over specified finite boxes; reconstructs all selected fixed-source cuts and their actual valuations; checks the finite spectra inside those cuts; samples the explicit occupancy inequalities on distinct ordinary orbit segments; and retains both post-cut counterexamples above. Those finite executions check algebra and implementation. They do not certify S-unit finiteness, supply a general numerical `H_S`, or prove the universal statements by enumeration.
