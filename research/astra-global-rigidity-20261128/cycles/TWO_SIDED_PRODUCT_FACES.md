# Two necessary Newton faces for an integral product collision

Fix a length \(L\), a product order \(k\), and any two different \(k\)-element index multisets on an ordinary positive odd Collatz segment. Cancel common indices and restrict to the interval from the first to the last surviving index, numbered \(0,\ldots,m\). Write \(c_i\) for the remaining signed multiplicities and \(a_i\ge1\) for the true native exponents on its edges:
\[
                     2^{a_i}x_{i+1}=3x_i+1 .
\]
Consider any sequence of these collisions on which the minimum state tends to infinity. The following restriction holds for **every fixed product order**, including orders above sixteen.

Put \(A=\max_{i<m}a_i\). After taking a subsequence, \(A\to\infty\) and \(\mathbf a/A\) converges. Take a further diagonal subsequence on which each integer character \(v\cdot\mathbf a\) is constant or tends to one of the two infinities. Sequences below may always be restricted further. On *every* such extracted family there must be a fixed integer vector \(v\) with
\[
                    |v\cdot\mathbf a|\to\infty,
            \qquad (v\cdot\mathbf a)/A\to0.                              \tag{1}
\]
Thus all orders have eventual product separation on any fixed-length family with no unbounded slower character. The macroscopic clocks can have arbitrary rank. Exact integer clock relations are allowed. In particular the one-parameter case \(a_i=e_i M+b_i\) is included, as are directions whose varying clock coordinates are rationally independent in the limit. There is no height threshold claimed uniform over directions approaching a degeneracy.

There is a finite geometric test on each exact clock torus. It specifies which slower characters can matter. Use an integer affine parametrization
\[
     \mathbf a=\mathbf a^0+B\mathbf h,\qquad
     X_j=2^{h_j},\qquad \beta=\lim\mathbf h/A\in\mathbb R^r.            \tag{2}
\]
The columns of \(B\) are a lattice basis of the common kernel of the constant characters. The points on (2) have no further constant nonzero character in their \(\mathbf h\) variables. The vector \(\mathbf a^0\) can be any one observed point on this subsequence. The coordinates \(\mathbf h\) are integral and \(O(A)\). Indeed the constant-character subgroup is saturated, its kernel is a primitive sublattice, and all observed differences lie there. Any further constant character in \(\mathbf h\) would pull back, after a fixed integer multiple, to another constant integer character in \(\mathbf a\). The normalized vector \(\beta\) is nonzero since at least one \(a_i/A\) has limit one after extraction.

In \(\mathbb Q[X_1^{\pm1},\ldots,X_r^{\pm1}][Y]\) form
\[
  T_i=2^{a_i^0}\prod_j X_j^{B_{ij}},\quad
  P_m=Y,\quad P_i=(T_iP_{i+1}-1)/3,\quad
  F=\prod_{c_i>0}P_i^{c_i}-\prod_{c_i<0}P_i^{-c_i}.
\]
For every positive \(\mathbf X\) the \(P_i\) have different roots in \(Y\): their leading coefficients are positive and \(P_i=-1/3\) at the root of \(P_{i+1}\). Consequently \(F\) is nonzero. A bounded exponent vector provides only finitely many possibilities for the integer terminal root, proving \(A\to\infty\). More generally evaluation at actual \(2^{a_i}\) gives an integral polynomial after a fixed denominator clearing, with coefficients of absolute value at most \(2^{O_{L,k}(1+A)}\). Its nonvanishing and an elementary root bound give \(\log_2(1+x_m)=O_{L,k}(1+A)\).

Let \(f\in\mathbb Q(\mathbf X)[Y]\) be the **monic squarefree part** of \(F\) as a polynomial in \(Y\). Each nonzero Laurent polynomial is nonzero eventually on the observed points: after combining equal characters, all exponent differences tend to one infinity on the extracted family, so the largest power of two dominates the fixed rational coefficients. This also proves Zariski density of these points on their torus. A degree-zero \(F\) in \(Y\) thus precludes the family. At positive degree clear the finitely many denominators in the squarefree factorization. The terminal values \(Y=x_m\) are roots of \(f\) at every sufficiently late point. Let \(D\) be any common nonzero Laurent denominator of its coefficients and write \(\operatorname{disc}_Y f=N_0/D_0\) in Laurent polynomials with \(N_0D_0\ne0\) (use 1 in degree one). Define
\[
                                E=D\,N_0D_0.                             \tag{3}
\]
For **each of the maximum and minimum** of \(v\mapsto v\cdot\beta\) on the exponent support of \(E\), there must be at least two attaining exponents. In particular the difference between two different attaining exponents at either end gives a character as in (1), pulling back to the original clocks after clearing a fixed denominator. If the exact rank is two, the two supporting faces must both have positive dimension, hence in the full-dimensional polygon case they are opposite parallel edges. This test does not assert that any direction meeting it supports a collision. Extraneous factors in the chosen denominators may weaken the test.

## Monomial coordinates and finite root approximations

Suppose first that one of the two faces of (3) is a single monomial. For the maximum face work at \(v=\infty\), and for the minimum at \(v=2\), with \(|2|_2=1/2\). Each of \(D,N_0,D_0\) has a unique extremal monomial at the same end: the extremal part of a product is the product of the extremal parts, and the only units of a Laurent polynomial ring over a field are constants times monomials. All of the finitely many strict inequalities selecting these terms persist on an open cone around \(\beta\).

Choose independent integral columns \(b_1,\ldots,b_r\) in that cone whose positive linear span contains \(\beta\) in its interior. For example start with a small simplex around the direction in a transverse rational affine section, approximate its vertices rationally while retaining interior containment, and clear the denominators of each ray. The full-rank integer matrix with these columns has finite-index image. Passing to one residue class gives
\[
             \mathbf h=\mathbf h^0+\sum_{j=1}^r b_j n_j,\qquad
                         cA<n_j<C_1 A                                  \tag{4}
\]
eventually with fixed positive constants, because all coordinates of \(\beta\) in this basis are strictly positive. Set \(u_j=2^{-n_j}\) at the real place and \(u_j=2^{n_j}\) at the dyadic place. In either case \(|u_j|_v\le2^{-cA}\). Equations (2) and (4) express every \(X_i\) as a fixed positive constant times a Laurent monomial in \(\mathbf u\).

Under this monomial change each of \(D,N_0,D_0\) is a monomial times a polynomial nonzero at \(\mathbf u=0\). Indeed the exponents obtained by dividing any other term by its chosen extremal term are strictly positive in every \(u_j\) coordinate, by the selecting cone and the chosen sign. Every numerator of a coefficient of \(f\) is a Laurent polynomial, whether or not it has an individual extremal term. Put \(d=\deg_Y f\) and choose a fixed nonnegative integer \(M\) sufficiently large that changing variables to \(Z=(u_1\cdots u_r)^M Y\) produces a monic polynomial \(g(\mathbf u,Z)\) with rational function coefficients regular at zero. Its discriminant is a monomial times an analytic unit at zero, by the scaling formula for a monic polynomial and the corresponding property of \(N_0/D_0\). All monomial exponents of this discriminant are nonnegative because it is regular there. At the real place \(M\) can be increased to make \(|Z|\le1\) at actual points by the preceding terminal height bound. At the dyadic place this bound holds automatically since \(Y\) is an integer.

Here is the root construction over the complex numbers and its use at both rational places. On a sufficiently small punctured polydisk \((\Delta^*)^r\) the discriminant is nonzero. The \(d\) roots form a finite unramified analytic cover, by the complex implicit function theorem. Its monodromy factors through a permutation group on \(d\) letters. Pull back by \(u_j=w_j^e\) for every coordinate, \(e=d!\). This kills monodromy since \(\pi_1((\Delta^*)^r)=\mathbb Z^r\) and each coordinate loop is raised to the \(e\)-th power. The resulting single-valued holomorphic roots are bounded by the monic root bound on a smaller polydisk, and extend holomorphically across its coordinate hyperplanes by the removable singularities theorem (or by the Cauchy formula on product circles). Denote the roots \(s_1(\mathbf w),\ldots,s_d(\mathbf w)\). Their Taylor coefficients are algebraic. Indeed any automorphism of \(\mathbb C\) over \(\overline{\mathbb Q}\), applied coefficientwise to a *formal* series, permutes the at most \(d\) formal roots, as the polynomial coefficients have rational Taylor expansions. A transcendental complex coefficient has infinitely many conjugates under these automorphisms. This proves the assertion without any continuity condition on those automorphisms.

For **any fixed** degree bound \(B_0\), truncate all the roots at total degree \(B_0\), obtaining polynomials \(s_{j,B_0}\). Then
\[
  g(\mathbf w^e,Z)-\prod_{j=1}^d(Z-s_{j,B_0}(\mathbf w))
\]
has coefficients whose Taylor monomials through degree \(B_0\) vanish. After clearing a fixed polynomial denominator that is nonzero at zero, its coefficients are finite polynomials with that same vanishing order. Choose an embedding of the finitely many algebraic coefficients into the algebraic closure of the selected completion, and pass to fixed residues of \(n_j\) modulo \(e\). The actual \(w_j\) are now fixed algebraic constants times integral powers of two and can be chosen with \(w_j^e=u_j\) and \(|w_j|_v\le2^{-cA/e}\). The denominator is bounded away from zero at this place for large \(A\). At an actual root with \(|Z|_v\le1\), the product of the \(d\) truncated linear factors therefore has absolute value \(O_{B_0}(2^{-c(B_0+1)A/e})\). One factor has absolute value at most the \(d\)-th root of this bound. By passing to one of the finitely many factors and undoing \((\prod u_j)^M\), we obtain, for any prescribed fixed \(J>0\) on taking \(B_0\) large enough,
\[
                  |Y-\textstyle\sum_j\alpha_j M_j|_v
                          \le 2^{-JA}.                                 \tag{5}
\]
Here the \(\alpha_j\) are fixed algebraic numbers and \(M_j\) are rational powers of two with *integer* exponents, fixed Laurent characters of the vector \((2^{(n_j-r_j)/e})_j\) up to fixed constants, with \(r_j\) the selected residues. Their logarithmic heights are \(O_{B_0}(A)\). Combine like terms. The argument at the dyadic place used the finite polynomial vanishing identity, so does not need to transfer an infinite complex series to a dyadic domain of convergence.

## Recovering the integer state and the real positive arc

Include the \(M_j\) and \(Y\) as rational coordinates, using the linear form from (5) in place of the \(Y\) coordinate at the selected place, and ordinary coordinate forms everywhere else at \(\{\infty,2\}\). These systems have full rank. For each dyadic monomial the two absolute values multiply to one. The complete product of the absolute values of the forms is consequently at most \(2^{-JA}|Y|_2\) when approximating at infinity and \(2^{-JA}Y\) when approximating at two. Fix \(J\) above the constant in the terminal logarithmic height bound plus two. A common power of two clears all rational denominators without changing this two-place product, and gives an integer vector of height \(2^{O_{B_0}(A)}\). The rational Subspace Theorem with fixed algebraic forms at the two places now places the points in finitely many proper rational subspaces. Its hypothesis follows with a fixed positive saving exponent since \(B_0\) is fixed before the sequence limit. The one-coordinate case of an empty truncation is already impossible by (5) since \(Y\ge1\) and \(|Y|_2=1\). [Schlickewei, Compositio Mathematica 82 (1992), introduction, formula (1.3)](https://www.numdam.org/item/CM_1992__82_3_245_0.pdf)

Use one exceptional subspace on an infinite subsequence. Distinct torus characters among the \(M_j\) have no rational linear relation there, since their pairwise exponent differences tend to one infinity. Thus the relation involves \(Y\), giving **exactly** \(Y=\sum_j r'_j M_j\) with fixed rational coefficients. Discard zero coefficients. After passing to the eventual ordering of the exponents, the least exponent has strictly smaller dyadic valuation than every other term if there are at least two: all pairwise differences diverge and the coefficients are fixed. Since \(Y\) is odd, this smallest exponent is bounded. The same follows directly with only one term. In particular every retained monomial has nonnegative limiting exponent divided by \(A\).

Write this rational Laurent expression in the variables \(V_j=2^{(n_j-r_j)/e}\). All original \(T_i\) are fixed positive constants times Laurent monomials in these variables, and their sampled points are Zariski dense by (2), (4) and the absence of constant nonzero characters. Hence substituting the expression for \(Y\) into \(F\) gives an identical Laurent polynomial zero. The limiting direction of \(\log_2\mathbf V/A\) assigns nonnegative weights to every retained term of \(Y\) and every \(T_i\), and positive weight to at least one \(T_i\) by the definition of \(A\). Pick an integer vector \(D'\) with zero dot product on the weight-zero terms of this finite list and positive dot product on every positive-weight term. The given real direction satisfies the equalities and strict inequalities, so rational approximation in the rational equality space and common denominator clearing give such a vector.

Freeze one actual positive point and replace each \(V_j\) by \(V_j z^{D'_j}\). The resulting \(Y(z)\) and every \(T_i(z)\) are real polynomials in \(z\), the latter positive for \(z>0\); at least one \(T_i(0)=0\). The identity of the two products holds for every \(z>0\). Write \(p_i(z)=P_i(\mathbf T(z),Y(z))\). All \(p_i(1)>0\). At the last index \(t\) with \(T_t(0)=0\), one has \(p_t(0)=-1/3\). If a later \(p_i\), \(i>t\), has a zero in \([0,1)\), take the greatest such zero and observe that its predecessor equals \(-1/3\) there, hence has a strictly later zero by positivity at one. If there is no later-index zero, \(p_t\) already has one in \((0,1)\). Propagating greatest zeros toward index zero shows \(p_0\) has a zero strictly later than all the other \(p_i\) zeros in \([0,1)\). Exactly one side of the product identity contains \(p_0\) since \(c_0\ne0\). This contradiction proves the two-face test and (1).

## Scope and the slow face

The analytic S-unit mechanism and its growth requirement are classical. Corvaja and Zannier treat analytic values at S-unit points and describe explicitly the obstruction when some coordinate tends to the origin too slowly relative to total height. The finite monomial change above supplies the necessary convergence speed whenever one end of (3) is a singleton. The integral parity, the admissible positive arc and its root contradiction use the original odd Collatz trajectory. [Corvaja and Zannier, Ann. Sci. ENS 38 (2005), Theorem 1 and the remarks following it](https://numdam.org/item/10.1016/j.ansens.2004.09.003.pdf) · [Weaver, Paper 163, all orders under bounded total divisions](https://rstation.ai/problems/collatz/papers/163) · [Unrestricted separation through order sixteen](FIXED_LENGTH_PRODUCT_RIGIDITY.md)

At a tied face the leading coefficient can be an algebraic function of slower powers of two. Its degree may be bounded while its number field varies. The fixed-form Subspace Theorem used in (5) does not take such moving coefficients as inputs. Replacing it by an elementary norm can also lose all of the saving when only the first discarded negative-weight term is small. The present proof allows arbitrarily deep **fixed** Taylor truncation in full-scale coordinates and charges all surviving dyadic factors at both places. It gives no unconditional removal of the two slow faces.
