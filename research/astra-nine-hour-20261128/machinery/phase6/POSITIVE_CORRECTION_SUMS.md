# Thirty-three ordinary first-hit correction sets contain a common interval in their sum

For a positive odd integer \(y\), let \(K_y\) be the closure of the real offsets of finite odd inverse words whose source first hits \(y\) at that word's terminal forward time. The empty word has offset zero. With \(I_e(y)=(2^ey-1)/3\), the offsets obey
\[
 R_{ew}=2^{-e}(1+3R_w).
\]
For **every** list of 33 positive odd integers coprime to three, repetitions allowed,
\[
 [0,33/64]\ \subseteq\ K_{y_1}+\cdots+K_{y_{33}}.                     \tag{1}
\]
The number of summands and the interval are independent of the supplied roots. This statement is elementary. In particular it requires none of the analytic hypotheses of the earlier length-zero theorem for the individual closed sets. Odd roots divisible by three have no odd predecessors and would have \(K_y=\{0\}\). [Definition and prior comparison, pinned PR 193](https://github.com/GettysburgResearch/collatz/blob/100ba857239a180840930765047d65ede92abeec/research/astra-six-hour-20261127/escape/CLOSED_CORRECTION_SUPPORT.md)

Keep only legal exponents \(e\ge3\) for which the child is another ternary unit. Of the three exponents of the required parity in every six consecutive integers, precisely one yields a child divisible by three: \(4\) cycles the three units congruent to one modulo three in \(\mathbb Z/9\). Thus the first exponent is at most six and successive legal exponents have gap two or four. Every retained step strictly increases the positive inverse value, so every retained finite word gives a genuine first hit. Every resulting mark is at most \(1/5\) since \(2^{-3}(1+3/5)=1/5\).

At an ordinary root \(x\) and an available exponent \(e\), take the closure of the marks of the empty word and all retained words beginning at exponents at least \(e\). Its interval hull is \([0,L]\) with
\[
                         2^{-e}\le L\le (8/5)2^{-e}.
\]
There are two closed subfamilies. One commits to \(e\), sending the full child set by \(r\mapsto 2^{-e}(1+3r)\). Its hull has length at least \((3/64)2^{-e}\), because the child's first legal exponent is at most six. The other skips \(e\), retaining zero and advancing the threshold to the next legal exponent. Its hull has length at least \(2^{-e-4}\). The two hulls are disjoint, since the latter ends at most at \((8/5)2^{-e-2}<2^{-e}\). Their extreme endpoints agree with those of the parent hull. Their length fractions are at least \(15/512\) and \(20/512\), so the intervening gap has length at most \((477/512)L\). These bounds persist under all preceding affine changes of coordinates.

Start with the 33 root hulls and fix a point in their interval sum. Initially every hull has length at least \(1/64\) and at most \(1/5\), so each has length at least \(15/512\) times the largest. Split a largest hull of length \(L\). The other 32 hulls have total length at least \(480L/512\), enough to cover the gap between the two new interval sums. Retain a child whose interval sum contains the fixed point. The relative lower bound is invariant, because each child has length at least \(15L/512\) and the new maximum is at most \(L\). The sum of the 33 lengths drops by at least \(15L/512\), as the omitted sibling has that length. Hence the successive maximum lengths tend to zero. Each nested coordinate hull shrinks to a point of its initial compact mark set: its endpoints already belong to that set. The chosen point is the sum of these limits. The initial hull sum contains \([0,33/64]\), proving (1).

For each unit root, the additive subgroup generated by \(K_y\) is therefore all of \(\mathbb R\). A probability measure having support \(K_y\) has characteristic function of modulus strictly less than one at every fixed nonzero frequency: equality would confine its support to one translate of a discrete subgroup. The argument gives only pointwise strictness. It uses the existence of branches, without lower bounds for their probabilities. A countably supported measure on a dense subset of \(K_y\) already has the same support and has purely atomic finite convolutions. Moreover the critical iid Syracuse measure in the Bellman question is on ternary units. Transferring a real support statement to its mass requires an additional joint-law estimate.
