## Physical clocks and the equal-base extension

For two integer sources differing by \(2^h u\), including \(u<0\), the first \(h\) parities agree. The endpoint difference is \(3^j u\), where \(j\) is the common odd count. This follows by induction at each common-parity step. Applying the identity on the preperiod and then on a lap proves the meeting at the two stated physical clocks. A zero displacement returns the same source. For odd 3-free \(w\), its only representation on a base at most \(w\) is the original \(3^r w\), since the source is odd and cancellation fixes the 3-free part.

For a positive phase \(C\) put \(D=2^e,\ S=3^p\) on a nonzero-length return. The lap product gives \(S<D\le4^p\le S^2\), with \(p>0\). The two sources are \(x=a+2^hDt=3^rw,\ y=a+2^hSt\). For the direct ternary representation \(y=3^s b\), put \(k=\min(r,s)\). The difference gives \(3^k\mid t\) since \(2^h(D-S)\) is prime to three. Substitution gives \(3^k\mid a\). The physical preperiod also gives \(0<a\le2^h C\).

If \(t>0\), these facts give \(D3^{2k}<Cx\). For \(r\le s\) this already exceeds \(3^{2r}\) on its right. For \(r>s\), \(Sx\le Dy\) and \(b\le w,\ w>0\) imply \(S3^r\le D3^s\). Squaring and using \(D\le S^2\) gives \(3^{2r}\le D3^{2s}\). Hence \(3^r<Cw\) in both cases. In particular, phase \(+1\) gives the strict conclusion \(3^r<w\) even when \(b=w\) and \(s<r\). Its strictness comes from the additive anchor \(a>0\). With \(t<0\), positivity forces \(D|t|<a/2^h\le C\). Here \(y>x\), so \(b\le w\) forces \(s>r\). Thus \(3^r\mid t\) and \(3^r<C\). In particular \(t<0\) is impossible at \(C=1\).

For negative phase \(-C\), the positive ordinary source \(x=2^{h+e}t-m\) forces \(t>0\). Its magnitude lap has \(D<S\), so \(y>x\) again forces \(s>r\) for \(b\le w\). Subtraction and substitution give \(3^r\mid t,m\). The magnitude bound \(m\le 2^hC\) and the displayed source equation give \(2^h\le w\) when \(3^r>C\). This proves \(3^r\le Cw\), including \(b=w\). Every \(h>0\) case on the full smooth cone has \(y\) odd because \(x\) is odd and \(x-y\) is even. Its halving exponent \(i\) in \(y=2^i3^s b\) is zero.

The phase-\(+1\) native drop uses \(aT^h(y)\le y\) and \(b<w\). An extension of that drop to arbitrary positive phases would be false. At \(a=1,\ h=1,\ C=2,\ e=2,\ p=1,\ t=2\) one has \(x=17,\ y=13\), both with ternary exponent zero. Yet \(T^3(17)=T(13)=20>17\). The phase-dependent scale inequality remains true. This control concerns the value at the meeting clock.

## Zero preperiod and full smooth cone

If \(h=0\), the signed anchor is the specified phase itself. Every nonzero periodic phase has magnitude prime to three. A nonzero integer cycle must have an odd step, and every state after that step is prime to three. Consequently \(3\nmid y\) in both signs, and a smooth replacement has \(y=2^i b,\ 0<b\le w\). For fixed target phase let \(E,P\) be the least period and odd count, \(q=2^E/3^P\), and let \(\ell\ge1\) count complete laps. Work in the rank-at-most-three subgroup of \((\mathbb Q^\times)^3\) generated by \((3,1,1),(1,q,q),(1,1,2)\). Its point is \((3^r,q^\ell,2^i q^\ell)\). The coefficients depend on the fixed \(C,w,b\), never on the exponents.

At \(+C\), eliminating \(t\) gives
\[
 (w/C)3^r+q^\ell-(b/C)2^i q^\ell=1,\qquad q>1.
\]
The two positive terms cannot cancel. A cancellation of the first and third leaves \(q^\ell=1\), impossible. A cancellation of the second and third leaves \(w3^r=C\), which fixes at most one \(r\) and would actually force \(t=0\). At \(-C\), elimination gives
\[
 -(w/C)3^r+q^\ell+(b/C)2^i q^\ell=1,\qquad q<1.
\]
The only possible cancellations involve the first term. Cancellation with the third would give \(q^\ell=1\). Cancellation with the second gives \(w3^r=Cq^\ell<C\), a finite range as claimed. In fact the latter equality is impossible because the right side has negative 3-adic valuation. All single terms are nonzero. The nondegenerate solutions are finite by Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt, with their number bounded in terms of dimension and group rank. The first coordinate records \(r\). Taking the finite union over \(b\le w\) and over the **prescribed target phases** proves the assertion. It gives neither a uniform location of the last exceptional exponent nor coverage by arbitrary varying target phases. [Primary theorem](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2002/155-3/p04)

The separate bounded-odd-work statement also uses this theorem correctly. At fixed \(j\) odd positions all \(j+1\) terms of the normalized affine identity are positive. Its coordinate vector is generated by a power of three in the first coordinate, a common power of two in every coordinate, and \(j\) independent powers of two in the remaining coordinates. Its rank is at most \(j+2\). Thus the number of exponents is uniformly bounded in the coefficients, since the first coordinate has 3-adic valuation \(r\). A variable target depending on \(r\) cannot be treated as the fixed coefficient \(y\).
